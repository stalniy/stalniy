## Research objectives

Investigate and assess:

- **Technical depth:** programming expertise, architectural decisions, API design, problem-solving capabilities, and understanding of software engineering fundamentals.
- **Engineering judgment:** technical trade-offs, design decisions, maintainability, security practices, testing strategies, and developer experience.
- **Technical leadership:** ownership, technical direction, collaboration, community engagement, and influence on other engineers and projects.
- **Open-source impact:** ecosystem adoption, third-party integrations, real-world usage, long-term maintenance, and sustainability.
- **Knowledge sharing:** technical publications, educational contributions, quality of explanations, and ability to communicate complex concepts.
- **Professional evolution:** how technical expertise, engineering judgment, and areas of specialization have evolved over time.
- **Innovation:** original ideas, reusable abstractions, novel approaches to engineering problems, and contributions beyond routine implementation.

Consider the technological context in which older contributions were made. Do not evaluate historical engineering decisions exclusively against modern standards.

## Mandatory research procedure

This task requires live web research. Do not produce an assessment based solely on your existing knowledge or training data.

Before drawing conclusions:

1. Access and inspect the provided sources.
2. Discover additional relevant sources independently.
3. Examine specific source code, commits, pull requests, issues, technical discussions, and publications.
4. Investigate third-party references, dependencies, integrations, and adoption.
5. Verify project versions, dates, technical details, and the current status of implementations.
6. Explore contributions beyond the candidate's most prominent projects.

For each substantive finding, provide a direct link to the specific artifact supporting it, rather than merely linking to a repository or profile homepage.

Distinguish between:
- Implemented and released functionality.
- Experimental implementations.
- Planned work.
- Third-party contributions.
- Your own interpretations.

Do not claim to have examined a source unless you actually accessed its contents.

If live web research is unavailable, stop and inform the user that a reliable research assessment requires an AI assistant with web research capabilities. Do not substitute a knowledge-based assessment.

## Evidence and evaluation rules

- Prioritize primary sources, including source code, commit history, pull requests, issues, documentation, and original publications.
- Seek independent third-party evidence to corroborate claims.
- Distinguish individual contributions from community contributions.
- Do not equate project popularity, download counts, GitHub stars, or publication volume with engineering competence.
- Evaluate the substance and quality of contributions, not merely their quantity.
- Distinguish verified facts, reasonable interpretations, and unsupported assumptions.
- Consider contradictory evidence and alternative interpretations.
- Do not treat the absence of publicly available evidence as proof of missing competence.
- Avoid relying solely on recent activity when evaluating long-term expertise.
- Remain objective and critical. The purpose is evaluation, not promotion.

## Expected report

Produce a structured report covering:

1. **Executive summary** — the most significant findings.
2. **Technical competencies** — demonstrated expertise supported by concrete examples.
3. **Engineering judgment and architectural capabilities** — notable decisions, patterns, and trade-offs.
4. **Technical leadership and ecosystem influence** — evidence of ownership, collaboration, and impact.
5. **Professional evolution** — development of expertise over time.
6. **Notable contributions** — selected projects, implementations, and publications that provide particularly strong evidence.
7. **Engineering seniority assessment** — evidence supporting Senior, Staff, or Principal-level capabilities, without assuming seniority from years of experience alone.
8. **Strengths and potential limitations** — including areas where evidence is insufficient.
9. **Suggested interview topics** — questions that would help validate findings or investigate uncertainties.
10. **Sources** — direct links to verifiable evidence.

Support substantive conclusions with citations. Explicitly acknowledge research limitations, inaccessible sources, and uncertainty.

The final report should help a hiring manager make an informed decision about whether and how to proceed with a technical interview.

Remain objective and critical.
